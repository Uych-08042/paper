## Draft

### Introduction

Visual object tracking estimates the location of an arbitrary target throughout a video from its initial bounding box. On unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) platforms, this task must be performed under rapid camera motion, large viewpoint and scale changes, frequent occlusion, and often small target appearance, while respecting the limited computation and energy available on board \cite{mueller2016benchmark,xue2025sglatrack}. Transformer-based trackers improve target--search interaction and global dependency modelling through encoder--decoder or one-stream architectures \cite{yan2021stark,ye2022ostrack}. Their repeated attention blocks, however, impose a substantial and largely fixed inference cost. This creates a persistent tension for UAV tracking: the representation capacity required by difficult frames must coexist with the low latency required for continuous deployment.

Adaptive computation provides a natural way to address this tension because video frames do not require equal amounts of processing. Early depth-adaptive tracking policies learned when to terminate a convolutional feature extractor \cite{ying2018depth}, while recent Transformer trackers bypass or activate blocks according to learned decisions \cite{yang2025abtrack,wu2025avtrack}. SGLATrack identifies representation redundancy and retains one candidate layer after a shared prefix \cite{xue2025sglatrack}; more recently, uncertainty-guided depth adaptation has used localization confidence and temporal continuity to control inference depth \cite{poggi2026unclstark}. These approaches show that tracking computation can be conditioned on the current video state. Nevertheless, their routing signals primarily describe representation redundancy, activation preference, or prediction uncertainty. They do not directly estimate the action-specific localization gain that an additional candidate computation will produce relative to a cheaper path. This distinction matters because an uncertain prediction may not be recoverable by additional computation, whereas a particular later representation may improve localization even when a generic confidence score is high.

Our diagnostic study exposes this mismatch in a frozen SGLATrack-DeiT* tracker. Under a shared-trajectory evaluation on UAV123, sequentially executing all twelve Transformer layers does not improve monotonically over the six-layer Fast path. In contrast, applying each of L7--L12 independently to the same L6 representation produces complementary predictions: the best candidate varies across frames, and oracle selection leaves a substantial gap over every fixed candidate. Yet the original SGLA selector collapses to the same candidate layer for all valid decision frames in this configuration. These observations indicate that the central decision is not simply whether a frame is difficult or whether a layer is redundant. Rather, the tracker must estimate which available action, if any, has positive marginal utility for the current tracking state.

This decision is further complicated by the closed-loop nature of tracking. A prediction is not only the output for the current frame; it also determines the search region presented to the tracker in the next frame. Consequently, counterfactual actions evaluated on a shared trajectory measure only immediate localization quality and can overestimate deployable performance. In our experiments, the Fast path appears markedly stronger when evaluated on states generated by SGLATrack than when it is allowed to generate its own subsequent states. Moreover, observable selection cues are weak in offline tests, while fixed candidate-box fusion policies fail to establish an advantage when run in closed loop. A useful adaptive policy must therefore be learned from causal, pre-candidate information and assessed on the state distribution induced by its own decisions.

Motivated by these findings, we formulate efficient UAV tracking as cost-sensitive action selection over a Fast path and six independent candidate layers. For each frame, the proposed utility-aware router predicts the marginal tracking utility of L7--L12 relative to the Fast path from the all-channel L6 representation, optional Fast-response information, and causal temporal state. It then selects the action that maximizes predicted utility after accounting for measured inference cost, retaining the Fast result when no candidate has sufficient expected benefit. To avoid test-set adaptation and trajectory leakage, utility labels are generated from disjoint GOT-10k or LaSOT training videos under both Fast and SGLA rollouts; ground truth is used only to construct supervision and never enters the routing features. The resulting policy is evaluated through closed-loop tracking and synchronized hardware latency rather than shared-trajectory oracle performance alone.

The principal contributions of this work are as follows:

1. We provide a controlled analysis of computation allocation in a layer-adaptive UAV tracker, revealing non-monotonic depth utility, complementary independent candidate layers, and a consequential gap between shared-trajectory and closed-loop evaluation.
2. We formulate adaptive inference as action-specific marginal-utility prediction and develop a cost-aware router that chooses between the Fast path and one independent candidate layer using only causal, pre-candidate observations.
3. We establish a leakage-controlled evaluation protocol that separates oracle potential, offline routing quality, closed-loop tracking performance, and measured inference latency.
